# Transport

The smallest interface in the system: move a sealed datagram from
one registered identity toward another. Everything it does not
promise is deliberately absent — durability belongs to the
[mailbox](./mailbox), attribution to the datagram's own signature
(P-ATTR.1), ordering to positions (P-LOG.2). That is what makes the
substrate replaceable: the v0 router, a relay mesh, or direct peer
links all satisfy the same contract.

## Operations

```
interface Transport
  send(d: Datagram) -> ()            -- fire and forget
  received() -> stream of Datagram   -- deliveries to the attached endpoint
```

### `send`

- **Pre.** `d` is well-formed; `d.from` is the caller's registered
  identity.
- **Post.** None. `send` is an attempt, not a promise — the push
  half of P-DELIV.2, under P-DELIV.3.
- **Refusal.** A Transport Provider MAY refuse to carry a datagram
  (quota, unknown recipient, policy under open question #2). What a
  refusal looks like is open question #9.

### `received`

- **Post.** Each item is bit-identical to what some sender submitted
  — a Transport Provider MUST NOT alter any field (alteration is
  detectable via `d.sig`), and MUST NOT interpret `payload`
  (P-STRUCT.1).
- **Consumer obligations.** The endpoint verifies and deduplicates
  every item (R-EP.2, R-EP.5).

## Non-guarantees

Delivery MAY fail silently and MAY duplicate; no ordering of any
kind is promised (P-DELIV.3). This is normative and load-bearing:
every reliability property in the system is built *on top of* an
unreliable transport, never assumed *of* it. A deployment whose
transport happens to be reliable gains efficiency, not different
semantics.

## Implementation note (non-normative)

v0: WebSocket sessions to the router; `send` is a socket write,
`received` the socket's inbound stream. Decentralized later: `send`
resolves the recipient's current locator and pushes peer-to-peer,
with relays as fallback. The contract is unchanged in both — the
two-substrate test applied to one interface.
